AI in UK Passenger Transport: Rail, Aviation and Maritime

Compensation is being automated as disruption rises. The regulators' own evidence shows that the passenger with an assistance booking is the one the system most often fails when a journey goes wrong, and the rules already insist on judgement for them.

Why this briefing

Disruption is rising and compensation has become a volume business. Rail operators closed 3.8 million delay compensation claims between April and July 2026, 55 per cent more than a year earlier, as cancelled trains rose by a third. In aviation, 51,691 disputes reached the approved dispute resolution bodies or the CAA in the year to March 2026, and in 45 per cent of those decided the passenger obtained a remedy.

The passenger with an assistance booking is the one the system most often fails when things go wrong. The rail regulator's own research found 86 per cent were met when their service ran normally and 67 per cent when their train was cancelled, and concluded that Passenger Assist "is more unreliable when services are disrupted". The aviation regulator reached the same view from different evidence.

AI is arriving at exactly that moment. The rail industry's AI plan sets out to automate compensation eligibility and already uses machine learning to screen Delay Repay for fraud; a search of its text finds no mention of disabled passengers. The EU is automating air compensation claims by law from October 2027. The rules already draw the line: rail redress for a failed assist must be decided case by case, and since February 2026 automated decisions based partly on health data, such as an assistance booking, are tightly limited.

Much of the AI already in use sits in the operation the passenger never sees. The rail industry plans one incident prediction shared across control, customer information, crew and rolling stock; predictive models for doors, HVAC and traction aim to cut avoidable cancellations; AI cameras detect trespass and passengers in distress; Heathrow is using AI on stand cameras to speed turnaround. British Transport Police publishes every live facial recognition deployment it makes at stations, with its outcome. The industry's own plan warns that many AI pilots struggle to produce measurable financial benefit.

The human moment

The passenger whose assistance booking does not survive the cancellation. A wheelchair user has booked help at both ends of a rail journey. The train is cancelled and the passenger is rebooked onto another service, by a person or by a system. Whether anyone is waiting at the other end depends on whether the booking travelled with them. The regulator's own survey shows that when trains are cancelled, a third of passengers with booked assistance are not met.

The discipline behind it

Every figure carries a source: the Office of Rail and Road, the Civil Aviation Authority, the Maritime and Coastguard Agency, the Department for Transport, official statistics, Parliament, a court, or an operator on its own record. Where the briefing reports an absence, such as words missing from the rail industry's AI plan or enforcement data not published, the source was searched for it.

The most quotable numbers in the sector are excluded on the record: an unattributed case study claiming 93 per cent automated claim processing and 70 per cent fraud-detection accuracy for UK train operators, and an airline's minutes-of-delay-saved figures given at a conference without a method.

Frequently asked

Questions people ask before reading

Is this vendor material?

No. Every figure traces to a regulator, official statistics, Parliament, a government department, a court, or an operator on its own record. No supplier performance figure is used, and the most quotable claims-automation figure in the sector is named as excluded.

Is it free to download?

Yes. It downloads directly, with no form and no email address required.

Is it critical of automated compensation?

No. Faster, automated compensation is a real gain for most passengers, and the briefing says so. The argument is that automation should be tested against the passenger it is most likely to drop, the one with an assistance booking when services are disrupted.

Does it name airlines or train operators?

Only where a regulator, an ADR body's published data, a court or a law-enforcement agency names them, or for what an operator says about its own systems. Airline dispute figures are used in aggregate.

Which parts of the UK does it cover?

Aviation and maritime figures cover the United Kingdom. Rail figures cover Great Britain, where the Office of Rail and Road regulates. Scotland's lifeline ferries are commissioned by Transport Scotland.

How current is the research?

Verified against primary sources as at 20 October 2026, including ORR statistics published in October 2026, the Department for Transport's aviation accessibility report of 19 October 2026, and both bills' stages in Parliament.

Does it cover AI beyond compensation and assistance?

Yes. A section covers AI across the operation: incident recovery, timetable and crowding models, predictive maintenance, trespass cameras, British Transport Police's published facial recognition register, Heathrow's stand cameras and the maritime regulator's AI strategy. No benefit figure is quoted for any of them, because none has a published evaluation.

What if I want to take this further?

The briefing ends with an eight-workstream action plan. The first three — readiness assessment, use case triage, and a regulatory and legal position paper — form a natural first engagement of six to eight weeks, enough to show what happens to an assistance booking when an automated tool acts.
