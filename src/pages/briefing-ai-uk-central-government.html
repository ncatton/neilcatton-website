AI in UK Central Government Services

Government's defence of its AI rests on the human in the loop. The human review is there. Whether it means anything is the question — and the review that demonstrably changes outcomes is the tribunal, months later.

Why this briefing

DWP describes its Universal Credit advances model as "the only fraud and error machine learning model currently deployed at scale in live service". Its own July 2026 assessment shows the model refers non-UK nationals at 3.35 times the rate of UK nationals — up from 2.27 a year earlier — while those referrals turn out to be correct at the same rate. DWP's own words: "the model is not working as effectively as it could for this cohort".

DWP's answer is the human review. "There is always a human intervention and decision." The caseworker is not told the model made the referral, and model referrals are mixed with random ones "to mitigate against human bias". That is a careful design, and it protects the review from the model. Nothing published shows whether the review catches what the model gets wrong.

Since 5 February 2026 the law has turned on whether human involvement in a decision is "meaningful". And under regulations in force since May 2026, the ICO is writing a statutory code on AI and automated decision-making that — through a provision few have noticed — courts and tribunals must take into account.

The human moment

The referral a caseworker never knows the model made. A Universal Credit claimant asks for an advance; a model flags the request; a caseworker decides, without being told why the case is in front of them. The review that demonstrably changes outcomes comes later: between April and June 2026, 60 per cent of benefit appeals decided at hearing were overturned in the claimant's favour.

The discipline behind it

Every figure carries a source: a department's own published assessment, the National Audit Office, the Public Accounts Committee, the Ministry of Justice's tribunal statistics, a tribunal decision, or the statute book. Where the briefing reports an absence — such as the asylum AI tools missing from the transparency register — every record on the register was read.

The most quotable number in the sector is excluded on the record: government's own £45bn a year from "full potential digitisation", whose methodology does not assess whether automation is feasible. A widely reported tribunal decision on a Home Office asylum refusal is used only as an illustration, attributed and stated as unproven, because it was not on the tribunal's published database.

Who it's for

A senior civil servant who has just picked up a department's AI programme, a member of an audit and risk assurance committee, or a digital leader asked to show that a department's human review is meaningful. It assumes government is well understood and AI is not.

Is the briefing critical of DWP and the Home Office?

It uses only what each department has published, what auditors and Parliament have recorded, and tribunal findings. DWP publishes an annual assessment of its model, which is more than most organisations do, and the briefing says so. The argument is about what is not yet measured — the human review — not about bad faith.

Which parts of the UK does it cover?

DWP's Universal Credit decisions cover Great Britain; Northern Ireland runs its own system through the Department for Communities, and Scotland's devolved benefits sit with Social Security Scotland. Home Office asylum decisions are UK-wide.
